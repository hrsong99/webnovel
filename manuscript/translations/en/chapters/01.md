# Chapter 1. What the Masters Left Behind

The new axe head rang.

Tongs in hand, Jin Cheol looked up. The lump of iron had not yet touched the quenching water, yet it trembled faintly atop the anvil. This was not the dull note of a misplaced hammer blow. The vibration had begun somewhere deep in the mountain, climbed through the smithy floor, and touched the iron.

“Father, are they blasting rock at the quarry again?”

His father, sorting charcoal beside the bellows, cocked his head to listen.

“They’re not working today. And if it were blasting powder, the ground would shake first. That sound is coming down from above.”

Before he finished speaking, every pair of tongs hanging from the eaves jingled at once.

Jin Cheol did not return the axe head to the forge. The iron was ready for the water now. The edge had ripened past persimmon-orange and was turning red as a cherry. A single breath’s delay would change its hardness. He glanced once at the mountain and once at the water trough, then adjusted his grip on the tongs.

“Staring down the iron again?”

Han Yeonhwa stood in the doorway, sunlight at her back and a roll of cloth cradled in both arms. She had spent half a year choosing it for their wedding quilt.

“I’m not staring it down. I’m reading the color.”

“That axe will harden from sheer fright of your eyes.”

Yeonhwa spread the cloth across the workbench. Jin Cheol spotted a crooked weft and pressed it with one finger.

“Here.”

“If your eyes are that sharp, you had better remember the color of my hair ribbon on our wedding day.”

“Red.”

“Last time you said indigo.”

His mother’s laughter burst from the inner room. Feigning defeat, Yeonhwa flicked the end of the cloth against Jin Cheol’s arm. On any other day, he would have laughed with her. But the iron on the anvil trembled again. This time the pulses came at regular intervals, like the slow passage of a shuttle through a loom.

Thump. Three breaths.

Thump. Two breaths.

The interval was shrinking.

Jin Cheol drew the axe head from the fire. When he plunged the dazzling iron into the trough, white steam billowed upward. Beneath the hiss of quenching metal ran the mountain’s rumble. As the two different vibrations collided, fine ripples crosshatched the water into diamonds.

“Yeonhwa, don’t go outside.”

“Why?”

A cheer erupted from the upper end of the village. Children raced toward the valley, shouting, “People are flying!” Jin Cheol’s father stepped over the threshold as well.

A man in white stood in the middle of the blue sky. Facing him was a giant of a man with a black saber resting on one shoulder. There was neither roof nor rock beneath their feet.

An old man dropped to his knees in the road.

“The Sword Saint of Mount Hua... Hero Wi Sohyeon.”

The swordsman offered a fist-and-palm salute. Though he stood far away, his voice came as clearly as if he were speaking beside their ears.

“Brother Namgung. Let us settle today the question we have carried for ten years. The sword places intent before all; the saber, momentum. Which comes nearer to the principles of heaven?”

Namgung Tae threw back his head and laughed.

“If heaven has ears, it will have its fill of listening today.”

There was no killing intent in either man’s words. They might have been old friends about to sit down over a game of go. That gentleness made Jin Cheol more uneasy, not less.

The sword left its sheath.

No one saw the first exchange. The pines on both sides of the valley bent in the same direction, and only afterward did the thunderclap arrive. The villagers’ cheers turned to screams. At the second exchange, stones poured from the cliff. Namgung Tae cut through the falling rock and swept his saber upward, leaving a white crescent hanging in the air.

Jin Cheol did not watch the duel. His gaze moved between the smithy pillars, the water in the well, and the tongs beneath the eaves. The valley was taking the two masters’ power and hurling it back. Strike a hollow piece of iron and its note swelled at particular points. Now the whole mountain rang like an empty bell. Each time the two men clashed, the vibrations did not fade. They accumulated.

“We have to get away.”

Jin Cheol seized his father’s arm.

“Where?”

He had no answer. The ground rang. The air rang. The mountain rang.

Jin Cheol started toward the valley. Yeonhwa caught him by the belt from behind.

“Have you lost your mind? What will you do up there?”

“Tell them to stop.”

“You think your voice will reach them?”

“If I strike iron—”

Jin Cheol snatched up the plowshare hanging outside the smithy and brought it down hard on the anvil. A clear clang spread through the village. He struck again. The villagers turned, but the two men in the sky did not. They possessed the power to shake a mountain. They had no reason to heed the ring of iron beneath it.

“Get down below the village!”

Jin Cheol struck the plowshare a third time and waved both arms at the spectators. The miller’s wife hesitated with her child in her arms. The tavern keeper only laughed.

“Will we ever see the like again in our lives? The great heroes know what they’re doing.”

“The cliff is throwing the sound back. It’s going to collapse.”

“And who knows the mountains better—you, or those two?”

One old man listened and pulled his two grandchildren close. The others could not tear their eyes from the sky. Each sweep of the Sword Saint’s blade scattered petal-like light through the air; each sweep of the Saber King’s weapon sent a hum shuddering through their ribs. It was beautiful. That made it harder to flee.

Jin Cheol ran for the meeting hall to ring the village bell. This time Yeonhwa did not stop him. She sprinted down the opposite alley.

“I’ll clear out the people by the stream. Wake the upper village.”

“Don’t come back to the smithy.”

“Since when are you in a position to order me?”

She snapped the words at him, then nodded at once. “Stay alive and nag me later.”

They split up. Jin Cheol hauled on the meeting-hall bell rope, but it snapped after the first peal. The vibrations descending from the mountain had already pulled the nails halfway out of the bell frame. He put his shoulder to the bell. The mass of metal tilted and sounded a heavy warning.

Doors opened at several houses. Jin Cheol shoved three children toward the stream path, then tried to take a lame old man onto his back. The old man dug in his heels, insisting he first had to loose his ox.

“You can buy another ox.”

“Without that beast, we die this winter!”

Jin Cheol smashed the stable bar. The startled ox charged into the alley, and the old man followed it, cursing all the way.

Then Jin Cheol heard his mother calling from the smithy. Yeonhwa, having cleared the homes by the stream, was returning through the back-wall alley as well. She meant to fetch Jin Cheol’s parents, who were still inside. Jin Cheol could not go downhill alone and leave them behind, so he turned back. Yeonhwa reached the outdoor work yard first, where a collapsed loom lay.

At the tip of Wi Sohyeon’s sword, light blossomed in branches like a plum flower. Namgung Tae caught it obliquely on his saber. A single line of white light lost its course and raked the valley wall.

The cliff split first.

Jin Cheol felt the catastrophe through the soles of his feet, not his head. The layered vibrations that had been building without cease suddenly vanished. Heated iron went quiet in just that way before it broke.

“Down!”

He shoved Yeonhwa into the outer yard on the far side of the inner room. She fell beside the loom. Jin Cheol reached for his parents, but his father stood before the inner-room door with his arms around his mother. The forge lay between them.

The world turned white.

Pressure came before fire. The smithy wall folded like paper. Nails tore free and roof beams flew. Jin Cheol was hurled behind the quenching trough. Its iron-bound sides crumpled and split the blast around him, while boiling water washed over his right arm and back.

Only after even sound had vanished did the pain arrive.

Jin Cheol opened his mouth in the mud. Ash entered instead of breath. The skin of his right arm had risen in dark red welts, and his fingers would not move. Clawing the earth with his left hand, he dragged himself free.

Where the smithy had stood, there was a black crater.

“Father.”

No voice came out.

Yeonhwa lay at the crater’s edge. Her left hand, still clutching the cloth, was trapped beneath the fallen loom. Jin Cheol crawled to her and pushed against the beam. It did not budge. He found a fallen iron bar, wedged it underneath, and heaved. Yeonhwa clenched her teeth and pulled her hand free. Her ring finger and little finger had been crushed beyond recognition.

“Your parents first.”

Yeonhwa forced the words out in broken breaths, but Jin Cheol bound her wrist with a strip of cloth. The bleeding would not stop. The living had to be tended first. Accepting that truth took an instant longer than a lifetime.

Below the smithy, those who had fled at the sound of Jin Cheol’s bell were returning. The miller’s wife carried her child on her back as she hauled stones away. The old man whose ox Jin Cheol had loosed smothered embers with a bloodied shovel. Jin Cheol entrusted Yeonhwa to them and climbed down into the crater.

He found his mother’s hairpin where the inner room had been. Its tip had melted and was still hot enough to adhere to his palm. Half of his father’s leather apron remained, pinned beneath a roof beam. Jin Cheol lunged to lift it, but his right arm buckled and he fell.

“Get the living out first!” someone shouted.

“They’re here.”

Jin Cheol gripped the beam again. No one could bring themselves to tell him that nothing recognizably human remained of his parents. Four people moved the beam together. Beneath it were only fragments of blackened bone. Jin Cheol wrapped them one by one in cloth. He did not ask which had belonged to his father and which to his mother. There was no one who could answer.

The houses by the stream had been empty because of the bell. The three children who had fled returned alive. When that gave Jin Cheol comfort, he felt he had betrayed his parents. When it did not, he felt he had turned even his choice to save those children into a lie. Unable to choose either feeling, he kept sifting through the ashes.

Near sunset, the two masters descended.

Not a fold of their robes was out of place. Namgung Tae surveyed the crater and the bodies and clicked his tongue.

“The valley’s resonance was greater than expected.”

He untied a purse of gold from his belt and set it upon a stone. It landed with a weighty thud.

“There is more than enough to rebuild your homes. I will see that you are paid without stint in the name of Namgung.”

Wi Sohyeon crouched before Jin Cheol, who was gray with ash. There was sorrow in the man’s eyes. For a moment, that made Jin Cheol wait for his words.

“My condolences,” Wi Sohyeon said quietly. “The insight gained today will preserve countless lives in years to come. Their deaths, too, will not have been in vain for the advancement of the martial path.”

Jin Cheol let none of it pass. It sounded like a judgment whose crime and sentence had already been decided—a judgment the dead could not testify against and the living could not refuse.

“If I go to the authorities, may I give them your names?”

Namgung Tae’s brows rose.

“May I have the record say this was a martial contest between two great heroes?”

Wi Sohyeon was silent for a moment. Then, looking at Jin Cheol’s burned arm, he said, “I will not prevent you from recording the truth. But do not let anger cloud that truth. The resonance was something neither of us could have avoided.”

Jin Cheol pointed to the bent nails around the crater and the stones shorn away on only one side.

“Who decides that it could not have been avoided?”

For the first time, Wi Sohyeon’s gaze lingered on Jin Cheol’s face. He did not grow angry. Instead, he answered with grave sincerity.

“One who has attained the martial path knows the power he can bear.”

“Then who bears what you could not?”

Namgung Tae stepped between them. “You talk too much for a man in your condition. I left ample gold for a physician.”

Again, gold answered in their place.

“Treatment must come first,” Wi Sohyeon said. “Do not let resentment damage your body as well.”

The two men departed before the sun touched the ridge. No one stopped them. No one so much as conceived of stopping them.

Yeonhwa was carried to a physician, and the villagers began gathering their dead. Jin Cheol sat alone before the purse left on the stone. He drew out one gold piece, slick with the evening light. It was worth more than his mother had touched in all her life. More than his father could have saved after making hundreds of farm tools.

He did not throw the purse away. With his left hand, he tied it shut and tucked it inside his robe. He could not destroy evidence left behind by a criminal.

High on the mountain, the sword scars and saber scars left by the two masters caught the sunset and shone. Jin Cheol measured with his eyes the distance from where those wounds began to where they ended.

“I will melt down your blades with this.”

There was no one to hear him.

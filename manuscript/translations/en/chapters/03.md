# Chapter 3. The Gate That Breaks Children

Over two years, Jin Cheol knocked on the gates of seventeen sects. Seventeen times, they shoved him back outside.

At first, he earned his keep as a blacksmith and petitioned for formal entry. They refused him because he was past twenty. Next, he repaired broken weapons for next to nothing and stole glances at the training yards. They told him his sense for qi was dull. In the end, he offered to do menial work for nothing but food and a bed, but one sect instructor saw the burn scars across his back and clicked his tongue.

“Your body is damaged already. No sect will waste medicine and rice on a laborer past twenty.”

What Jin Cheol wanted to see was who made martial artists—and by what process.

In the spring of his twenty-first year, he gained entry to Cheongu Hall, an affiliate of the Unmun Escort Agency. Not as a disciple, but as an itinerant blacksmith hired to mend pots and blades. For fifteen days, he counted the breaths he heard every dawn. The third-rate disciples drew six long, shallow breaths, then paused for one. Whenever Do Changsu, a martial artist in the late third-rate realm, crossed the training yard, all those rhythms broke at once.

Do Changsu was a hearty man. When he brought over a broken saber, he paid an advance, and when his subordinates bowed, he greeted them by name.

“Don’t hone it too sharp. A blade guarding cargo should frighten a man before it cuts him.”

He flicked the saber’s spine with a fingernail. It rang clear, and he smiled in satisfaction.

“You know iron. But what happened to your back? Struck by a thunderbolt?”

“The smithy collapsed.”

“Tch. Hard world. Still, you’re lucky your hands kept their skill.”

By his words alone, he sounded like a compassionate escort guard. Yet he never once paid a wage when he used a village forge, and if a household fell behind on the protection fees levied in Cheongu Hall’s name, he emptied its grain jar. Even the advance he had bestowed on Jin Cheol so generously had come from someone else’s winter stores.

That night, a letter arrived from Yeonhwa. The survivors of Cheonggye Village had built a treadle loom in Mulle Valley. Its pedals changed the tension without requiring her left hand to grip the warp, and she could drive the shuttle with her right hand alone.

The matter of their wedding had been settled before the opening lines of the letter. Two years ago, Yeonhwa had written that she would not break their betrothal, but neither would she set a date. She would not remain behind as someone waiting while Jin Cheol wandered. They would continue the engagement only after deciding anew what home they would share and what authority each would have to stop the other. Jin Cheol had sent back his assent. Ever since, their letters had spoken not of marriage, but of work to be done.

A column of numbers always closed Yeonhwa’s letters: the balance of their common fund, newly reported victims of martial contests, the number of people requiring treatment. This time, there was one unfamiliar entry.

*Three households sent children away after paying initiation fees to an Unmun Escort Agency affiliate. None of the children returned.*

Jin Cheol folded the letter and slipped it inside his underclothes.

Before entering Cheongu Hall, he had visited two of those three households. The parents in the first believed their son was training at a distant branch of the escort agency. Every year, they paid a fee to visit him. They had never once seen his face. At the second house, all that remained was the child’s straw sandal, sent back by the sect.

“They said he had no talent and ran away,” the boy’s elder sister said. “But he was so afraid of the dark he couldn’t even go to the privy alone. They expect us to believe he crossed a mountain road by himself and fled?”

Instead of answering, Jin Cheol examined the sandal’s sole. Hardly any mud had worn away. Inside the toe was a dark, rusty stain. It looked less like the shoe of a child who had run than a sandal forced back onto the foot of a child who could no longer move.

The parents in the third household refused to speak. They planned to send their second child to the Unmun Escort Agency as well. To admit that something had happened to their first would be to admit that both the initiation fee they had already paid and their first child’s suffering had been for nothing.

Jin Cheol wrote back to Yeonhwa. *I will find out where the children disappear.*

Her second letter was brief. *Do not confuse finding out with rescuing them. Do not play the hero alone and lose the records too.*

He read it and packed the tools for opening a cellar lock separately anyway. He told himself that he needed to know a structure before he could tear it down. He did not tell Yeonhwa that he meant to charge in if he heard a scream.

Three days later, seven children arrived at Cheongu Hall. The youngest looked as though not all his front teeth had grown in. Their parents set silver coins and grain before the gate and bowed again and again. The instructor clapped each child on the shoulder.

“If you have the gift, you’ll become one of the Unmun Escort Agency’s own. No more fear of hunger. No more fear of bandits.”

By that evening, the children were gone.

Carrying the saber whose repair he had deliberately delayed, Jin Cheol headed for the inner quarters. The gatekeeper blocked his path, then stepped aside when Jin Cheol said it was Do Changsu’s saber. At the end of a corridor came a faint sound of weeping.

From beneath the floor.

A new lock hung on the cellar door. Jin Cheol drew a slender chisel from the toolbox on his back. He listened to the lock’s steel plates slide into place. Two pushes, and the bolt released.

The air below smelled of water and blood.

Seven great wooden tubs stood in the middle of a stone chamber. The children lay submerged, their hands and feet bound. At measured intervals, the instructors hauled them up by the hair, allowed them one mouthful of air, then thrust them under again. Incense burned along the wall. The pounding of a drum filled the darkness.

“If you want to live, open the place below your navel!” an instructor shouted.

One child vomited water and called for his mother. Another already hung limp. The burn scars on Jin Cheol’s right arm remembered boiling water from long ago. His fingertips stiffened before the rest of him did.

He vaulted into a tub and dragged the nearest child’s head above the surface.

“What do you think you’re doing?”

Jin Cheol cut the ropes with his chisel. An instructor’s cudgel crashed into his back. White engulfed his sight, but he shoved the child out of the tub. The second blow buckled his knees.

From the doorway, Do Changsu sighed.

“Blacksmith, you should have kept to your own work.”

“They’ll die.”

“Some will.”

Do Changsu nodded as if stating a perfectly ordinary calculation.

“But if even one opens, that child’s whole family will eat for ten years. Their parents knew when they sent them. You think the world outside will raise these children gently?”

“If they knew, then you won’t mind sending them back.”

Jin Cheol shielded the child behind him.

“Do you have any idea what I paid for my own initiation? I gave Cheongu Hall three years before they even taught me to hold a blade. Don’t kick away another man’s ladder just so you can feel righteous.”

“You’re drowning children to recoup your initiation fee?”

“Who in this world isn’t under someone else’s heel?”

Do Changsu pointed to the old scar beneath his chin.

“They buried me in a coffin for five days. Every time I failed to stop crying, they blocked the air hole. Three of us went in. I was the only one who came out. Only then did they send rice to my family. I must look like a monster to you. But my mother survived the winter on that rice.”

“Because it happened to you, it has to happen to these children?”

“If I don’t do it, someone else will. At least I give them a few more breaths.”

One instructor beside the tubs looked away. In that instant, Jin Cheol understood that Do Changsu’s words could be both excuse and truth. Someone crueler might indeed take his place. Do Changsu was also taking his share. Neither truth erased the other.

“Then today, I’ll be the one who gives them another breath.”

Jin Cheol pushed the child farther behind him.

Do Changsu’s thumb pressed against the saber guard.

The first strike smashed into Jin Cheol’s ribs. He blocked with an arm, but the force drove through to the bone. He never saw the second. His shoulder struck the stone wall. His breath stopped; blood flooded his mouth.

On one hand and both knees, Jin Cheol listened.

Before Do Changsu attacked, he drove his right heel into the stone floor. A short inhalation, then an exhalation as the scabbard shot forward. Inside it, the saber blade took up the breath and thrummed low. The rhythm was strangely like the drumbeat that had sounded just after a child swallowed a scream underwater.

Two, one, pause.

Two, one, pause.

Do Changsu’s third blow came. Jin Cheol twisted half a beat early. The scabbard tore across his ear instead of crushing his temple. As he fell, he flung the hammer from his toolbox—not at Do Changsu’s foot, but at the tip of the scabbard.

Clang.

The instant the hammer struck the blade, Do Changsu’s breath caught by the slightest measure. His wrist wavered. The gap lasted less than a single breath. For Jin Cheol, it was not enough. His body could not follow.

Do Changsu’s foot drove into his belly. Jin Cheol flew backward, overturning two tubs. Water and children spilled across the floor.

“Stop!”

Do Changsu roared at the instructors. “Get the children out first! Are you trying to kill all the merchandise?”

Those words brought the children out of the water. Even as Jin Cheol coughed blood, he turned one child’s face sideways and let the water drain from the mouth.

Then the smell of burning paper drifted down from above. Smoke filled the stairs. Someone outside shouted that there was a fire. As the instructors milled in confusion, a gray-bearded man descended the steps. He wore neither an official’s robe nor martial garb, but the worn coat of a coroner.

Jin Cheol would later learn that Seo Muhan had spent three days filing through the bars of the back-alley drain, one by one, and had arranged for Madam Obok, an herb carrier, to ready an empty cart. He had not known how many children he might get out, but he would not open a door without preparing both a route and someone to receive them. The smoke from wet mugwort was rising from that same cart.

“I’m here to collect the Unmun Escort Agency’s inspection records.”

He flashed a badge. In the moment Do Changsu narrowed his eyes, the old man sent the three children who could walk up the stairs. Waiting above, Madam Obok received them one at a time and led them out through the rear door of the inner quarters.

“That design of badge was retired ten years ago,” Do Changsu said.

“Sharp eyes. If your nose were as good, you’d know it’s wet mugwort burning, not ledgers.”

The old man kicked out the torch on the wall. Darkness swallowed the cellar.

“Blacksmith! Crawl away from the sound!”

Three children remained below. Jin Cheol hoisted one who was still breathing onto his back. The old man hooked the other two under his arms and dragged them. In the darkness, Do Changsu’s saber finally left its sheath. The blade’s ring gave away its position. Jin Cheol hugged the wall and moved away from the sound.

Three children escaped by the stairs. Jin Cheol and Seo Muhan dragged the other three through the widened drain. All six living children were waiting in Madam Obok’s cart by the time they counted them. She stripped off their wet clothes and wrapped the smallest in her own outer coat. When the child opened his eyes and asked where they were going, Seo Muhan said they would recover at a farm to the north. There he would learn what debts and dangers waited at each home, then ask whether they wished to return or choose another place to live.

“I couldn’t save one,” he said.

The child who had already gone limp in the tub. Jin Cheol tried to reach out, but his arm would not rise.

“Where will the others go?”

“If we send them straight back to their parents, they may only be sold again. Once they wake at the northern farm, I’ll ask what the children want first.”

“Who are you?”

“Seo Muhan. Once, I priced weapons for the Imperial Bureau of Artifacts. Later, I priced the lives of the people those weapons killed.”

He cut open Jin Cheol’s clothes and examined his injuries. At the sight of the burn scars across his back, his hands stopped. He pressed along the scars’ edges with his fingers and counted Jin Cheol’s breaths. His expression changed like that of a man finding familiar script in an old record.

“Your dantian nearly opened as well.”

Jin Cheol wiped his bloodied lips.

“I have never learned martial arts.”

“That is why it closed. You never learned how to keep tearing it open.”

Seo Muhan looked back toward the alley where the dead child remained.

“But something else opened in its place. You hear the discord in weapons whenever someone circulates qi.”

That day, Jin Cheol climbed into Seo Muhan’s cart with the children he had saved. Do Changsu had seen his face; there was no road back to Cheongu Hall. As the cart turned out of the alley, a saber point scraped once along the rear axle, but none of them noticed.

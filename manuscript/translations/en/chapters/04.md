# Chapter 4. The Wound Remembers Power

Seo Muhan’s morgue lay beneath an abandoned dyehouse.

Twelve days had passed since their escape from Cheongu Hall. For the first four, Jin Cheol could not even rise from his bed at the northern farm. Seo Muhan bound his bruised but unbroken ribs in a broad strip of cloth and allowed him short walks only after he stopped coughing blood. Once the fevers of the six children broke, they too were moved to two separate places. Three chose to remain with Madam Obok. The other three wanted to stay together, so they went to another farm in the victims’ network.

Only later did they discover the trail Do Changsu could follow. When his saber scraped the cart’s rear axle on the night of their escape, blue resin used by Cheongu Hall to mark horseshoes had lodged in the cut. Seo Muhan did not catch the scent and scrape it away until they reached the farm. The mark had led their pursuers as far as the northern road, and Do Changsu’s men had questioned the apothecary from whom Seo Muhan had borrowed three great vats. Seo Muhan moved the children first and only then brought Jin Cheol to the morgue beneath the dyehouse. Even that refuge would not hold for long.

They shifted three huge vats crusted with dried blue dye. An iron door emerged in the floor. Below it, the air smelled of medicine and lime. More weapons than human bones hung along the walls: a saber with a missing notch, a spear snapped through the middle, an iron shard burst apart from within. Each object bore the name of the dead and a date.

Jin Cheol looked at the saber before the corpse laid out on the table. A series of half-moon cracks marked its spine.

Five interlocking rings had been stamped beneath the hilt. The same mark had been scratched with a needle into one corner of the corpse’s name tag. One drawer under the table was shallower than it appeared, and when Jin Cheol tapped the bottom, thin metal rang within. Seo Muhan told him they would finish the examination first and left the drawer locked.

“The saber did not break first,” Jin Cheol said.

Seo Muhan stopped midway through rolling up his sleeves.

“Without even looking at the body?”

“There’s no blood inside the cracks. Someone struck and broke it after its owner fell.”

“The Murim Alliance coroner wrote the opposite. According to him, the weapon shattered and the fragments lodged in the man’s chest.”

Seo Muhan drew back the cloth. Dark red blotches spread across the corpse’s lower abdomen. Fragments of the saber were buried in the chest, but there had been little bleeding. The wounds had been inflicted after the man was already dead.

“He died while circulating qi. The wounds outside the body came afterward. It began here.”

The old man pointed below the navel.

Jin Cheol frowned. “I was taught that the dantian is not an organ in the body.”

“That is true. Cut open the belly and you won’t find a pouch you can hold in your hand. Yet when that place is damaged, blood and flesh collapse in its wake. It leaves traces too consistent to call it nonexistent.”

Seo Muhan took a thin sheaf of papers from a drawer. They were records of sect initiates. Eight years old, suffocated underwater. Ten, imprisoned in darkness. Nine, falsely told that both parents had died. Half the children who survived complained of pain and fever in the same place below the navel. Only those taught a breathing method within days developed their first internal energy.

“You mean ambient qi seeps in where the mind has been torn?”

“That is my hypothesis.”

“Plenty of people are wounded. Not every war orphan becomes a martial artist.”

Seo Muhan almost smiled.

“I have waited twenty years for that question.”

He opened another set of records. They had been divided in meticulous detail: the age at which the shock occurred, how long it lasted, the victim’s breathing immediately afterward, even the density of qi in the surroundings.

“Not every wound becomes a dantian. The body must be strong enough not to die, the mind shaken so deeply that it can scarcely return—and at that very moment, the victim must draw in ambient qi with measured breaths. Sect initiation rites turn chance into a procedure.”

Seo Muhan set three bowls of water on the table. The first went beside the corpse, the second before Jin Cheol, and the third at the foot of the stairs. He scattered charcoal powder over each surface, then asked Jin Cheol to straighten the corpse’s rigid hand into the posture for qi circulation.

“Does qi remain in a dead man?” Jin Cheol asked.

“His internal energy disperses, but for a time, the wound’s pull leaves a trace.”

When Jin Cheol extended the dead man’s fingers, only the charcoal beside the corpse gathered, ever so faintly, toward the center of the bowl. Jin Cheol held his breath, then immediately asked to exchange the second bowl. Perhaps the first bowl had an uneven bottom, or perhaps the table’s vibration had caused it. They switched the bowls three times and disrupted the corpse’s posture as well. The same pattern appeared only when they straightened the lower abdomen and placed the hands in the posture for qi circulation.

“A visible phenomenon does not prove your explanation,” Jin Cheol said. “Heat left inside the body could be moving the air. It may not be a wound at all.”

“Exactly. That is why I call it a hypothesis.”

Seo Muhan indicated a bundle of records on the wall: the results of repeating the same experiment on nineteen other corpses. The higher a dead martial artist’s realm had been, the longer the charcoal continued to gather. On corpses with damaged dantians, it scattered outward instead.

Jin Cheol did not trust every figure. He marked the lines in which the temperature of the morgue and the size of the bowls differed. Rather than take offense, Seo Muhan circled them in red ink.

“Many people doubt another man’s convictions. Few doubt even the answer they need to hear. Keep doing that.”

“Then all that talk of discerning talent is a lie.”

“Only half a lie. They call it talent when a child tears easily and survives after being torn.”

Footsteps sounded on the stone stairs. Jin Cheol seized his toolbox by reflex. The door opened, and Yeonhwa descended. Even with two fingers missing from her left hand, the knots securing the bundle of books she carried had not shifted.

“How am I supposed to find you when you summon me and then hide under a vat?”

Seo Muhan coughed, abashed. “If I write a detailed address, the men coming to arrest me find it more easily too.”

Yeonhwa looked first at Jin Cheol’s split lip and the bandages around his ribs. She came closer, but did not touch the wounds.

“The children?”

“Six are hidden at the northern farms,” Seo Muhan answered. “One has been placed in a coffin.”

Yeonhwa entered the numbers in the ledger. Six of seven. Even when recording the number who had lived, she did not erase the one who had died.

At her waist hung the field copy of the Mulle Valley common ledger. The original rested in an iron chest at the watermill, its keys divided among three households. Every month, they made two copies of the victims’ names and the accounts of money received and spent. The bundle Yeonhwa had brought held copies and letters from each village. Neither a single fire nor a single blade could erase every name.

After hearing Seo Muhan’s explanation, her first question was different.

“If it is a wound, can it be closed?”

“The soul tries to heal. Training is much like tearing open the same place every day.”

“Then they need only stop the breathing method.”

“A newly opened dantian can heal that way. But once enough qi has accumulated, it blocks the wound’s path to closure from within. Seal it by force, and the trapped qi surges backward through the meridians and organs and kills the person.”

Yeonhwa’s gaze went to the corpse. “Like this man.”

Seo Muhan did not answer.

Jin Cheol examined the saber’s cracks again. A faint powder, unlike the oil, clung inside the blade. He scraped some free with a fingernail and smelled it.

“There is a sedative herb on this. Who stopped this man’s qi circulation?”

Seo Muhan’s shoulders sagged.

“I did.”

The lamp flame in the morgue leaned to one side. The old man straightened the corpse’s name tag.

“He wanted to leave his sect. I tried to stop his breathing practice and let the dantian heal. I knew I had to drain the accumulated qi first. I opened the meridians with needles and channeled the qi into his weapon. When I thought half of it was gone, I administered medicine to soothe the wound.”

“It wasn’t half.”

“I measured only the force that emerged through the saber. I failed to count the qi steeped into his bones and organs.”

Yeonhwa asked, “How many people did it take before you stopped?”

“The first and the last.”

“Why were you researching it before then?”

Seo Muhan took a long time to answer.

“My son entered a sect when he was eleven. Three days later, they sent his body home. They said he lacked the talent to endure the rite. I drew an imperial salary, so I made no trouble. Instead, I cut open other people’s bodies to learn why. I told myself it was for my son. In truth, I used the dead to cover the cowardice that had kept me from asking while he was alive.”

Jin Cheol did not comfort the old man. A confession of failure would not restore the dead. But he did verify that not a single line of Seo Muhan’s records had been marked as a success. The man had not erased results that contradicted his own beliefs.

“If we discover how to close a dantian, we can end the murim world.”

At Jin Cheol’s words, Yeonhwa turned at once.

“Whose dantian?”

“Those who want to lay down their power, first.”

“And after that?”

“Those who stand upon other people’s lives.”

“Even if they refuse?”

Jin Cheol saw Do Changsu’s scabbard pass over the children’s heads again.

“If Do Changsu comes to buy another child tomorrow, do I need his permission first?”

Yeonhwa’s voice dropped. “If you close that man’s dantian, who feeds the family held hostage by the escort agency? Take away his power, and you take away his livelihood—and his family falls with him. Don’t forget that the men you hated wrote a person’s price and a house’s price in the same column.”

Their argument broke off at the sound of metal above the door.

Once. Twice. Three times. The bar across the dyehouse entrance being cut apart.

Seo Muhan extinguished the lamp. “Do Changsu.”

“How do you know?”

“That saber point left resin on the cart. Once he narrowed the search to the dyehouse quarter, he would inspect the vats one at a time. And cutting a bar in three strokes to open the door without damaging the records was Cheongu Hall’s method too.”

Do Changsu’s voice rang from above.

“Old Seo, six children are worth a fortune. Hand over the records, and I’ll send the blacksmith back with his arms and legs attached.”

They swept their eyes across the morgue. The iron door was the only exit a standing person could use. Low on the wall was a passage once used to carry corpses out, barely broad enough for a single person to crawl through; beyond its rusted cover, it joined the alley drain. A small bellows and brazier stood against one wall. On the opposite side hung a pulley threaded with a thick hemp cord. Yeonhwa pulled the cord once and judged its tension.

“Wind this twice around that post.”

“What are you planning?”

“I’ll show you where the force goes when a single warp thread snaps on a loom.”

Jin Cheol poured charcoal into the brazier. Seo Muhan worked the bellows until the fire revived. Jin Cheol propped the broken spears and sabers from the wall along the brazier’s rim. There was no time to heat them. He knew instead what notes lengths of iron would make.

Do Changsu opened the iron door and descended. Four Cheongu Hall martial artists followed him.

“The three of you have been amusing yourselves digging graves.”

The instant his foot touched the last stair, Jin Cheol struck the longest spear shaft with his hammer. A piercing note whirled through the cramped room. He followed it by striking a short saber, a cracked cauldron, and an iron plate in succession. Do Changsu’s subordinates clapped their hands over their ears.

Do Changsu did not. He gathered force below his navel, endured the sound, and charged straight in. Jin Cheol remembered his breathing. Two, one, pause. But the body attempting to evade him was still an ordinary man’s. The scabbard grazed his shoulder and tore the flesh.

“Now!” Yeonhwa shouted.

Seo Muhan tore the wedge from the pulley. The taut hemp cord whipped free and struck the shelves. Scores of weapons crashed to the floor at once. Do Changsu’s feet became trapped amid the iron.

Jin Cheol split a sack of lime above the brazier with his hammer. The powder met the hot air and spread like a white cloud. The three of them retreated toward the entrance to the narrow corpse passage.

Behind them, Do Changsu swung his saber. Internal energy carried along the edge split the brazier apart. Burning charcoal scattered into the shelves of records.

As the firelight licked beneath the table, the false bottom of the shallow drawer warped upward. The edge of a metal plate stamped with five rings appeared. Jin Cheol pried up the bottom with a chisel and pulled the plate free. Beside it lay a thick research journal compiling Seo Muhan’s hypothesis. He could not carry both in one hand. After confirming that Yeonhwa ahead of him had tucked the bundle of name tags inside her clothes, he left the unverified journal in the flames.

The passage had barely enough room for one person to crawl. Before entering, Yeonhwa bound the field ledger beneath her clothes. She pulled herself ahead with her right hand while Jin Cheol gathered her skirts from behind so they would not catch around her feet. Seo Muhan refused to release a bundle of autopsy records separate from the common ledger, and his shoulder wedged against the wall.

“Leave it!” Yeonhwa shouted.

“It holds the children’s names.”

Jin Cheol cut the knot around the bundle. They rolled the pages a few at a time and divided them beneath all three of their clothes. Seo Muhan discarded the remaining calculation sheets and kept the later pages recording the names of the dead and the course of each failed attempt. It was a choice made while the fire spread to the passage mouth.

When Seo Muhan tried to turn back, Jin Cheol caught his arm.

“You’ll die if you go.”

“Those are twenty years of records!”

“If you die, only the failures will remain.”

Grinding his teeth, Seo Muhan crawled into the passage. By the time the three emerged through the alley drain, fire was rising between the floorboards of the dyehouse.

At rain-soaked dawn, they hid from their pursuers in an abandoned potter’s kiln. The pages were scorched in places, and blood had begun to seep anew from Jin Cheol’s shoulder wound. Yeonhwa opened the field ledger and made certain it was dry, then wiped clean the palm-sized metal plate Jin Cheol had taken from the fire. There was no writing on the front. Five rings biting into one another were engraved on the back. Along the edge, small characters emerged.

*Blackwind Mountain. Archive of Forsaken Obsessions.*

Seo Muhan’s hands trembled. Only now did the mark on the corpse’s saber and name tag acquire a meaning.

“The thing he hid to the end must have been... a repository sought by those who wished to abandon martial arts.”

Jin Cheol took the metal seal, still warm. The home to which he might have returned and the morgue they might have preserved had both burned.

He slipped the seal into his robe. Beyond the fire, torches carried by Do Changsu’s men searched the alleyways.

“Let’s go to Blackwind Mountain. Before they finish cooling the ashes.”

They could not climb the mountain at once. During the ten days that an old woman potter let them use the empty kiln, Seo Muhan stitched Jin Cheol’s torn shoulder and watched for the bruising at his ribs to subside. Jin Cheol shouldered a pack only on the day he could lift his arm as high as his shoulder. In the meantime, Yeonhwa sent word through the victims’ network dividing the six rescued children among their refuges, and dispatched copies of the autopsy records in two directions. At dawn on the eleventh day, after the torches of their pursuers had disappeared, the three took the western road toward Blackwind Mountain.

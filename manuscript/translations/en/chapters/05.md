# Chapter 5. The Archive of Forsaken Obsessions

When the black silver grass along the ridge bent beneath the wind, all of Blackwind Mountain looked drenched in spilled ink.

Jin Cheol and his companions climbed the ridge for three days. The ribs Do Changsu had battered throbbed with every step, and the burn scars on his right arm stiffened whenever the cold wind touched them. Each time Seo Muhan lost the trail, he held the metal seal close to the rocks. They followed whichever direction set one of its five rings faintly trembling.

Yeonhwa was the first to notice what was wrong.

“The wind is coming from the left, but the grass is leaning right.”

A narrow gap opened between the stalks of black silver grass. Wind was being drawn into the mountain. The three of them pulled away a curtain of vines and found an iron door set into the cliff.

Instead of a keyhole, five round metal plates overlapped upon the door. One was bronze, one iron, one silver, one tin, and the last a dark red metal Jin Cheol could not name. Beneath them, a sentence had been engraved.

*Place the strongest metal first to gain power. Place the weakest first to find the path.*

Seo Muhan reached for the silver plate, but Jin Cheol stopped him. The plates were bolts meant to slide, one by one, into five slots beside the door. Tin dust remained in the first and deepest slot. The farther outward the other slots lay, the heavier the scratches left by hard metal passing through them.

“It isn’t asking us to rank them from strong to weak.”

“Then what is it asking?”

Jin Cheol removed all five plates and flicked their edges with his fingernail. He put the tin plate in the first slot, deepest within the door. Silver followed, then bronze, then the dark red metal. He stood the iron plate in the outermost slot last.

“‘First’ means the order in which the plates are fitted. The outer iron takes the impact across a broad face. The soft tin inside deforms and swallows what remains. Whoever opened this before us left scratches in the same order.”

The notes of the five plates merged into one, and the iron door swung inward.

The Archive of Forsaken Obsessions did not smell of books. Thin sheets of metal covered every wall and every span of floor. Each footfall returned from far away. Dust lay thick, yet there was not a single cobweb.

The inscription worn into the first room’s threshold read: *The Resonance Chamber That Returns Obsession.* Hundreds of names had been carved into the walls. Beneath each name stood a sect, a cultivation realm, and one last wish.

*Yu Jagyeong of the White Cloud Sect. Late second-rate. I want to lay down my sword before my daughter.*

*Jang Munak of the Blood Saber Brotherhood. Early first-rate. I wish to stop dreaming of the faces of those I killed with my own hands.*

*Jeonghae, a lay disciple of Shaolin. Mid third-rate. I want to return to my fields.*

A red line had been drawn through most of the names. Beside each was the cause of death: executed for betrayal, suspected of leaking a secret manual, qi deviation staged to conceal murder. This was not a register of those who had died seeking power. It was a register of those killed for trying to abandon it.

Yeonhwa copied the names into her ledger one by one.

“You mean to write down all of them?” Jin Cheol asked.

“They died because their names were erased.”

As she copied the last one, the point of her iron stylus touched a narrow groove beneath the nameplate. A loom shuttle sounded within the chamber on the heels of her words.

Yeonhwa raised her head. There was no loom in the archive. Yet the shuttle passing between the warp threads rang clear, as did the creak of a treadle and her mother’s voice scolding her over a mistaken knot. The blood drained from Yeonhwa’s face.

Jin Cheol heard a hammer.

Once. Twice. His father’s habit of striking just off the center of the anvil. His mother setting down the evening meal and asking when the wedding would be. It was all so perfect that opening a door seemed enough to carry him back to the afternoon before the disaster.

The metal walls trembled and reflected the forge around him. His father stood beside the bellows. His mother was smiling. No one had died yet. Jin Cheol held a red-hot axe head in his hands.

This time, he could hear the warning in the mountain sooner. If he ran now, he could drag his parents out. He could send Yeonhwa outside. Perhaps all four of them could hide behind the quenching vat.

Jin Cheol took one step.

“Into the water, son. The iron’s cooling.”

His father had spoken.

Jin Cheol could not lower the axe head into the vat. He wanted to hear exactly how the iron had rung that day, to see the pattern the ripples had made. If he watched just once more, perhaps he would understand what he had missed.

A palm cracked against his cheek from behind.

Yeonhwa had struck him. Her right hand glowed red.

“Listen to the living, not the dead.”

Jin Cheol looked at her. For a moment, all five fingers appeared whole on Yeonhwa’s left hand. Then the light across the metal wall wavered, and five became three again.

“I could do it over.”

“No. You could only watch it again.”

“This time I might save them—”

“Then go.” Yeonhwa clenched her teeth. “Live happily ever after inside the memory you made. I’ll go outside with my three fingers and count how many living people need food today.”

The roar of the disaster approached the illusory forge. Jin Cheol turned his back on his parents. On a block before the metal wall lay the dents left by earlier visitors’ hammers, beside a catch that would never reset once broken. As he listened to his parents die again, he brought his hammer down upon the block.

The catch snapped. The chamber’s resonance shattered. His father’s voice stretched thin and vanished, and the forge became a cold wall once more. Behind them, the iron door slammed down and sealed the way they had entered.

Yeonhwa, too, broke free of the loom’s sounds. Seo Muhan was on his knees in a corner, apologizing to a child no one else could see. Only after Jin Cheol and Yeonhwa seized an arm apiece and hauled him up did the old man open his eyes.

“The price of passing was the road back,” Seo Muhan said hoarsely.

“Those who left the hammer marks on that block must have paid the same.”

The next door was open.

At the center of the inner archive rested a single book, its thin iron leaves bound together with thread. Four characters had been cut deep into its cover.

*The Sutra of Unraveling.*

Jin Cheol distrusted the title at once. If the manual offered greater power, it had no place among the names outside. The author’s warning had been inscribed on the first iron leaf.

*You who would unravel power, do not touch it while ignorant of power. Closed eyes cannot see the grain. A slow body cannot reach the grain. Empty hands cannot move the grain.*

Only three branches of training were described in detail in the opening chapter: the Mind’s Eye, which sensed the broad flow of qi; Unraveling Qi, a neutral form of internal energy refined from ambient qi so that it bore the nature of no sect; and the Form-Shifting Art, which moved the body half a step beyond the point of collision. The first footwork of the Form-Shifting Art was called Drifting Step. At the stage of Perception, Unraveling Qi remained within the practitioner’s body and did nothing more than support the senses and posture.

Along the edges of the iron leaves, the names of four higher stages continued like seals: Analysis, Interference, Synthesis, Dissolution. The annotations Seo Muhan deciphered were unambiguous. Analysis, which read the structure of techniques observed repeatedly, required a second-rate body. Interference—sending Unraveling Qi outside the body to touch another person’s qi circulation—must not be attempted before the first-rate realm. The final chapter, Dissolution, could be opened only after the bodily transformation of the transcendent realm.

Jin Cheol read the passage twice. Relief and despair came together. This was no effortless miracle. Even if he began today, he would still be slower than Do Changsu’s saber, and reaching Wi Sohyeon might take not years but a lifetime. Yet neither could anyone steal one book and immediately strip people of their dantians.

“Why are you smiling?” Yeonhwa asked.

Only then did Jin Cheol realize that the corner of his mouth had moved.

“At least it doesn’t lie that I’ll become a master overnight.”

“This doesn’t sever cultivation,” Jin Cheol said.

Seo Muhan turned an iron leaf. “It is a martial art meant to reach cultivation severance. It says one must attain the bodily transformation of the transcendent realm before the soul’s wound can be safely unraveled.”

“Then it could take decades,” Yeonhwa said.

“He might never reach it at all.”

Jin Cheol read the first page of training. Perception began by making a small wound in a closed soul. Unlike common initiation methods, which tore that wound open through terror, this method found the grain of a scar already formed and consciously spread it apart. Ambient qi entering through that gap then had to be filtered through the breath and refined into Unraveling Qi.

The wound in him had begun to open at Cheonggye Village, then healed. The book said people bearing such a trace were rare, and reopening it might return them to the pain of the day it had formed.

Yeonhwa closed the book.

“You don’t have to do this.”

“I do.”

“Until a moment ago, you were condemning people who tore children apart to turn them into martial artists.”

“There is a difference between someone else tearing me open and my choosing it.”

“The wound won’t know that you chose.”

Jin Cheol had no answer. Heat from the disaster crept up his back. His body wanted him to stop breathing and close the book. His hands refused to let it go.

He opened the iron leaves again.

“Then you record it. Write down why I began and where I went wrong. If I go wrong, make me stop.”

Yeonhwa did not agree at once.

“If I tell you to stop, will you?”

“I’ll hear your reasons.”

“You still mean to be the final judge.”

“That is all I can promise now.”

Yeonhwa glared at him for a long time. Then she opened her ledger to a blank page.

“I am not giving you permission to train. I can’t stop you, so I am imposing conditions. I keep the original iron leaves. You copy only the first chapter. Seo Muhan keeps the resonance implement except while you are training. Before you use it against anyone, he and I, along with a majority of the people harmed, must give our consent.”

“And if I break those conditions?”

“We cut you off from the common fund, lodging, and information network, and we take the manual back. If you still insist on going on, you do it alone.”

Seo Muhan nodded as well. Jin Cheol pushed the original Sutra of Unraveling toward Yeonhwa.

“I’ll begin on those terms.”

Jin Cheol sat down for his first session of training. Seo Muhan guided him through the opening steps, then withdrew. The book was the only master this martial art had to offer.

Jin Cheol did not force his breaths deeper. He recalled the vibration that had passed through the anvil just before the disaster, then searched for a resonance deeper within. The burns on his back grew hot. Beneath his navel, a pain as fine as the point of a needle stirred.

If he opened the door, the afternoon his parents died was waiting.

Jin Cheol did not turn away. The roar, the boiling water, and the smell of burning flesh returned in order. He gave none of them a name. He counted: three in, hold for two, out for five.

The wound that had healed shut parted by the smallest degree.

The mountain’s cold qi pressed through the gap. When he grasped at it greedily, the pain sharpened. He shortened the end of his inhalation and lengthened the exhalation. Heat and cold dispersed, leaving beneath his navel a single strand fine as a hair, bearing the nature of neither.

Unraveling Qi.

It was neither warm nor cold, yet sweat soaked Jin Cheol from head to foot. He braced his hands on his knees and vomited. When he opened his eyes, the world looked unchanged. He could sense only the metal seal’s tremor, at once in his palm and below his navel.

“You haven’t reached even the entry of the third-rate realm,” Seo Muhan said, fingers on his pulse. “The flow breaks after three breaths.”

Yeonhwa wrote down his words exactly. *One strand of Unraveling Qi. Held for three breaths. Vomited once.*

Only when Seo Muhan permitted it did Jin Cheol bring out the three nails he had recovered from his parents’ remains. He revived a small brazier in one corner of the archive and bound the nails to a single ring without deadening their different notes. It was no weapon. It was a training implement, a means of testing which nail’s tremor he sensed first while circulating Unraveling Qi within his body.

The first resonance implement was nearly finished when a carrier pigeon flew in from below the mountain. Yeonhwa unfolded the note tied to its leg. It had come from Mulle Valley’s information network.

*Do Changsu demands double the protection fee. In three days, he will take grain and one child.*

Yeonhwa’s face hardened.

Jin Cheol assumed the first stance of Drifting Step, though he could not yet walk it properly. His ribs ached after one step, and the Unraveling Qi scattered almost at once. Do Changsu was late third-rate. If Jin Cheol faced him now, he would likely end up on the ground again.

“We can make it down in three days.”

“When will you train?” Seo Muhan asked.

“I’ll be breathing on the way.”

The three of them left the Archive of Forsaken Obsessions before sunrise. The original manual traveled in Yeonhwa’s pack, the resonance implement separately in Seo Muhan’s medicine case. Jin Cheol carried the copy of the first chapter inside his robe. With every step, he breathed to keep the newly opened wound from closing.

Do Changsu was late third-rate.

Jin Cheol’s Unraveling Qi could not yet last even three breaths.

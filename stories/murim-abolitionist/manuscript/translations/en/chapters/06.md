# Chapter 6. The First Crack

A broken loom leg had been driven into the ground like a stake at the entrance to Mulle Valley. From it hung the blue banner of the Unmun Escort Agency, marking a village that paid for protection.

Beneath the banner sat twelve sacks of grain and a twelve-year-old boy named Gangho.

Yeonhwa ran to him. “Who told you to sit here?”

“My mother.”

The boy did not cry. He repeated, as if he had learned the words for an examination, that the escort agency would feed him and teach him martial arts. Fresh crescents marked the inside of his wrist where his mother’s nails had dug in while she tried not to let him go.

Jin Cheol seized the banner pole. Yeonhwa caught his wrist before he could tear it from the earth.

“You pull that down, the fight starts today. Prepare first.”

“They mean to take him today.”

“And when you rush in and get beaten down first, who stops them?”

He let go.

During the three-day descent from Blackwind Mountain, he had kept his breathing matched to his steps until the thread of Unraveling Qi lasted ten breaths. That was barely enough for Seo Muhan to call him an entry-level third-rate martial artist. The sutured flesh at his shoulder had scabbed over, but he still could not twist hard through the ribs Do Changsu had battered. Every attempt at Drifting Step widened into the forge stance his body had known for years.

The Sutra of Unraveling had changed him. It had not changed the odds.

Do Changsu arrived at sunset with two subordinates, though he entered the village alone. When he saw Jin Cheol, his thumb nudged the hilt of his saber clear of the scabbard.

“So you did attach yourself to old Seo.”

“Leave the child.”

“Our agreement says that if the protection fee comes up short, we may take payment in people.”

Yeonhwa stepped forward with the ledger tucked under her arm. “Who signed that agreement?”

“The former village head.”

“He died at Cheonggye Village. You intend to take a living child under a dead man’s seal?”

Do Changsu’s eyes settled on the ledger. He did not bother pretending to smile. “I don’t enjoy this. But if I miss this month’s tribute, my sister and nephew are thrown out of the agency’s inner compound. If that is all they suffer, they will be lucky. I still owe the debt for my initiation.”

“Why should this village repay your debt?” Jin Cheol asked.

“Who else will?” Do tapped two fingers against his dantian. “Debt flows downhill. I’m being crushed from above too. You spent enough time outside sect gates to know that no one gives away martial arts. I was beaten and starved from thirteen onward to open this. If I retreat now, those years disappear, and so does my family.”

When he finished, he tightened the knot on Unmun’s banner twice. His hands had paused only while he spoke of his sister and nephew.

“Give us two days,” Yeonhwa said. “I’ll set what you collect beside what the Unmun Escort Agency actually receives.”

“And what good will that do?”

“It will separate what the village owes from what you stole.”

His face chilled. “Still quick with figures after losing two fingers.”

“I’m quicker. I can’t afford mistakes with what remains.”

Through all of it, Do Changsu watched the ledger. He granted the two days and took a room at the village inn, posting a subordinate by the window to watch anyone carrying papers in or out. He noticed Jin Cheol following at a distance, but an opponent newly admitted to the third-rate realm earned no more than a snort.

For two days, Jin Cheol studied the traces Do left rather than the man himself. Water at the well drew inward whenever Do held his breath. After someone refused him, his left hand always pressed the scabbard before his right foot moved. The body of a late third-rate martial artist was too fast to chase, so Jin Cheol stopped trying. Between the well and the weaving room, where a wall denied the saber room for a broad stroke, he marked a half-step in the dust and practiced turning on his heel. His ribs endured one attempt in twenty.

Yeonhwa covered a courtyard wall with the common ledger and Unmun’s receipts. In two years, Mulle Valley had surrendered seventy-eight seom of grain. The agency acknowledged forty-one. Twelve seom of the missing amount had been charged as interest on Do Changsu’s initiation debt; Cheongu Hall and the escort guards between Do and Unmun had divided the rest.

She sent copies down three roads. The network of nine villages harmed by the same system sent back a tally promising to release the testimony of the six rescued children. The copy addressed to Clerk Bak Munsu produced nothing. Their runner reached the magistrate’s gate and returned empty-handed. The Cheongha Escort Agency, a rival to Unmun, also stepped back from its earlier interest: unless the magistrate stamped the filing, it would not ask the three merchant companies to move their contracts.

“If we had trusted all three,” Yeonhwa said, laying the unanswered packet beside Cheongha’s hedged reply, “we would be waiting for two answers while they carried off one child.”

One route remained alive, and the figures were already in front of the villagers. People whose grain Do had seized stood beside neighbors who had once asked his subordinates to drive bandits from the road. The same protection could be real and still be used to strip them bare.

“Starting today, no household pays alone,” Yeonhwa told them. “If they draw steel, then we do not take the beating alone either.”

Seventeen households owned a hammer or a loom shuttle. They would not use the tools as weapons. At an agreed rhythm, they would strike stone and walls, drowning the breathing cadence Jin Cheol had memorized. Those same seventeen households heard the exact limits on the resonance implement: it would only help Jin Cheol find his timing; he must stop at the agreed signal; and no one had consented to using it for any other purpose. Each household pressed a handprint to Yeonhwa’s page. Only then did Seo Muhan open his medicine case and release the three-nailed ring from his custody.

Do Changsu returned at sunset on the second day with six men. The ledger on the wall stopped him before the villagers did.

“Take that down.”

One subordinate stepped forward. The villagers closed the way, sickles and hoes in hand but held low.

Yeonhwa raised a copy above her head. “This went to nine victim villages, Clerk Bak Munsu at the magistrate’s office, and the contracts office of the Cheongha Escort Agency. Bak did not answer. Cheongha wants an official stamp before it risks anything. But the paper reached them, and nine villages already hold the children’s testimony. Tear down this copy, and that fact spreads first.”

A man named Bak Seok left Do’s line and approached the wall. Beneath the name of his father, Bak Nosik, he read the same entry twice.

*Funeral expenses: three nyang of silver. Interest charged after repayment of principal: seven nyang.*

“The agency gave us one nyang for my father’s funeral.” Bak Seok looked back. “Where did the rest of this debt come from?”

Another subordinate, Yu Gap, found his sister Yu Wol’s name. Two nyang for medicine had become a nine-nyang initiation deposit. When Do ordered them to rip down the ledger, Yu Gap lowered his saber hand.

“Explain my sister’s debt first. I won’t touch this paper.”

Do bared his teeth. “Get it off the wall. I’ll forgive both initiation debts.”

Bak Seok never looked away from his father’s name. “You’ll forgive debts that never existed?”

He too stepped out of the road. The four remaining men glanced from their comrades to the villagers, and none drew a saber.

Do Changsu leveled his scabbard at Jin Cheol. “So the first thing you learned was how to hide behind others.”

“Today I don’t take the beating alone.”

Do drove off the ground.

Hammers answered from every quarter of the village. Loom shuttles cracked against walls—bang, bang, then a beat displaced, a pattern that promised order and denied it. Do’s first breath disappeared under the noise.

Jin Cheol watched the left hand pressing the scabbard and the turn of the right heel. At Perception, Unraveling Qi could do no more than brace his senses and posture from within his own belly and legs. Nothing passed from his body. Outside him, only the physical vibration of hammers, nails, stone, and steel moved through the village.

The Mind’s Eye caught the broad course of Do’s internal energy as it rose from below his navel, crossed the right side of his waist, and climbed toward the shoulder. Perception did not announce a weakness. But Jin Cheol had watched this motion more than twenty times, and just before the qi reached the shoulder, Do’s left ribs opened.

Half a step took Jin Cheol out of the completed stroke.

Not far enough. The scabbard passed before his nose and smashed into the wall, spraying stone across his cheek, but its edge clipped his shoulder and numbed the arm. Do kicked off the wall and turned faster than Jin Cheol’s body could answer. The return blow found the old bruises along his ribs. Bone cracked inside him, Unraveling Qi broke apart, and the rim of the well struck his back.

“This is the martial art you stole?”

Do drew his saber. There was no blade aura along the edge, but internal energy made the steel hum low in the hand.

Swallowing blood, Jin Cheol took out the resonance implement. Three old nails hung from one crude ring. It could neither stop a saber nor bring down a man. He pressed the ring to the well stone and flicked it once.

The bent nail rang first, answering the saber’s note.

That sound gave him his marker. Hammers and shuttles crashed louder as Do abandoned his hearing and used internal energy to steady his center. The well water puckered inward.

Two, one, pause.

Jin Cheol moved before the blade came free. He was not faster; he had chosen the shortest road. Ducking under the edge, he planted his left foot outside Do’s right. For the first time, the opening step of Drifting Step held.

An elbow crashed down across his back. Pain tore through the burns and darkened his sight, but he did not retreat. From inside his robe he drew a short chisel, set its point beneath the saber guard, and braced the blunt end against the well’s rim. Iron bridged stone, chisel, saber, and wrist.

When the bent nail trembled hardest, a heavy hammer struck the well’s foundation stone.

The shock traveled through stone and metal into the bones of Do Changsu’s wrist.

Do’s fingers opened by reflex, then closed again. That tiny correction collided with the last count of the cadence Jin Cheol had memorized—*two, one, pause*. Do sent internal energy into his arm half a beat too early. His wrist, not yet locked, received the force of his own body.

The saber fell into the well.

Do clutched his wrist. The numbness would soon pass. Neither his meridians nor his dantian had been harmed; his qi circulation was already searching for its rhythm.

Jin Cheol did not put the chisel to his throat. He kicked the fallen scabbard away instead.

“Give us the tribute ledger.”

“You won’t kill me?”

“If you die, your debts disappear with you. So do the names of the people above you.”

As Do’s qi circulation recovered, Jin Cheol’s legs gave out. His chest struck the corner of the well, and the cracked rib finally broke. Seventeen villagers filled the alley behind him. Bak Seok and Yu Gap had taken their places in front of the wall, not at Do Changsu’s side.

Even without his saber, Do could have killed Jin Cheol. He looked first toward the weapon at the bottom of the well, then at the ledger, then at the two men who had lowered their blades. His gaze lingered on the tally from the nine villages. When it reached the entries for his sister, Do Jeongran, and his nephew, Do Yun, a muscle jumped in his jaw.

The spaces reserved for the magistrate and Cheongha were still empty. But if he cut down seventeen villagers now, everyone could see what would fill them next: bodies, testimony, names carried along nine roads, and four subordinates forced to choose whether to join him before witnesses.

His foot shifted toward the well. Then it moved half an inch back.

Do Changsu pulled a small book from his robe and threw it into the dust. “Do not touch my sister or my nephew.”

Yeonhwa picked it up. “Their names go in a different column from their debts.”

Unmun’s blue banner came down. The twelve sacks returned to the common storehouse. Yeonhwa asked Gangho whether he wanted to go home with his mother or first meet the six children staying at Obok’s northern farm. Holding his mother’s hand, he chose to meet them. He would decide where he wanted to live and learn after he had seen the place for himself.

Jin Cheol had a broken rib, and the Unmun Escort Agency still stood. But from then on, Mulle Valley would no longer be stripped one household at a time.

Some villagers wanted to burn the agreement with Unmun. Yeonhwa stopped them. An unjust contract still preserved the names and seals of everyone who had enforced it; they would need those in the next fight. The paper went into an iron chest in the common storehouse, and three different households each took one key. No single person would own what the village had won.

A new rule was fixed behind the old agreement.

*No child may be surrendered for debt, protection fees, or initiation fees. A child who returns chooses where to live and where to learn.*

The six children at the northern farm sent their own decisions. Two would return to their families once those families’ false debts had been struck from the ledger. Four would remain with Obok for the time being. The village pledged grain and witnesses to enforce both choices. After meeting them, Gangho would be asked in the same way. Seven living children, seven choices no creditor would make for them.

Do and his men withdrew empty-handed. Before crossing the pass, he looked back once. There was less resentment in his face than warning: a greater creditor was coming behind him.

That night, one villager proposed pinning Do Jeongran’s and Do Yun’s whereabouts beside their entries on the wall.

“We need to know where they are if we mean to keep Do Changsu from returning.”

Yeonhwa took the paper down and folded it. “I promised their names would not be treated as debts. If the agency casts them out, Mulle Valley sends two mal of grain first. Their location stays in the inner record inside the iron chest.”

Agreement did not come easily. Someone asked whether the village would still feed an enemy’s family if another child failed to return—another child like Hajin. Gangho’s mother placed one mal from her own share on the floor. The common storehouse supplied the second. They would hide Do Changsu’s family, if hiding became necessary, without hiding what he owed or what he had done.

Later, Yeonhwa bandaged Jin Cheol’s injuries. She wound the cloth with her right hand and held the knot steady with the three fingers left on her other. When he tried to lay his hand over hers, she put the medicine bowl into it.

“If you could have destroyed Do Changsu’s dantian forever today, would you have?”

The answer rose at once and stopped behind Jin Cheol’s teeth. Do’s power crushed people below him. It was also the only livelihood holding his sister and nephew inside the agency compound. Taking it might reduce one set of victims and create another.

“I don’t know.”

Yeonhwa pulled the knot tight. When he groaned, she eased her hand a little.

“I’ll write that down. If you ever start saying you know too easily, I’ll show it to you.”

***

Hundreds of li away, Cheon Muryeong laid three reports side by side in the Murim Alliance records archive: the disturbance at Cheongu Hall, the dye-house fire, and Do Changsu’s account from Mulle Valley. Seo Muhan, a burned blacksmith, and repeated sounds of struck metal overlapped. The three nails and the failure in Do’s qi timing appeared only in Do’s statement, so Cheon marked them separately rather than turning one witness’s claim into common fact.

In the final column he wrote not a conclusion, but a hypothesis.

*An external physical stimulus produced by the same individuals may be connected to Do Changsu’s failed qi timing. These reports do not establish that Unraveling Qi—or any energy projected outside the body—was involved.*

He tied red thread around the name Mulle Valley, took up his investigator’s badge, and left for the south before dawn.

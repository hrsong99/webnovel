# Chapter 5. The Archive of Forsaken Obsessions

When the black silver grass along the ridge bowed to the wind, all of Blackwind Mountain looked drenched in spilled ink.

The climb took three days. Every step woke the bruises Do Changsu had left along Jin Cheol’s ribs, while the burn scars on his right arm drew tight whenever the cold reached them. Whenever Seo Muhan lost the trail, he held the metal seal near the rocks and waited. One of its five rings would begin to tremble, so faintly that Jin Cheol had to watch the old man’s fingertips to see it, and they would follow the direction it chose.

Yeonhwa found the entrance first.

“The wind is coming from the left,” she said, “but the grass is falling to the right.”

A narrow seam divided the black stalks. Air hissed through it, drawn into the mountain. They pulled away a heavy curtain of vines and uncovered an iron door set flush into the cliff.

There was no keyhole. Five round metal plates overlapped at its center: bronze, iron, silver, tin, and a dark red alloy Jin Cheol could not name. An inscription ran beneath them.

*Those who place the strongest metal first shall gain power. Those who place the weakest first shall find the path.*

Seo Muhan reached toward the silver plate. Jin Cheol caught his sleeve.

The plates were not ornaments but sliding bolts, each fitted to one of five slots beside the door. Tin dust clung to the deepest slot. Farther out, the grooves bore progressively heavier scratches from harder metal.

“It isn’t asking us to rank them.”

“What, then?”

Jin Cheol removed the plates and flicked each rim with his thumbnail. He slid the tin into the innermost slot, followed by silver, bronze, and the unknown red metal. Iron went last, across the outside.

“The outer plate takes the blow. The softest one gives and absorbs what reaches the center.” He brushed tin dust from his thumb. “Someone else found the same answer.”

The five notes joined. Deep inside the cliff, a metal lock disengaged, and the door swung inward.

The Archive of Forsaken Obsessions did not smell of books. Thin metal sheet covered the walls and floor, returning each footfall from some impossible distance. Dust lay thick enough to hold their tracks, but not a single cobweb crossed the corners.

Worn characters on the first threshold named the room: *The Resonance Chamber That Returns Obsession.* Hundreds of names had been cut into the walls. Beneath each stood a sect, a cultivation realm, and one final wish.

*Yu Jagyeong of the White Cloud Sect. Late second-rate. I want to put down my sword before my daughter.*

*Jang Munak of the Blood Saber Brotherhood. Early first-rate. I want to stop dreaming of the faces of those I killed.*

*Jeonghae, lay disciple of Shaolin. Mid third-rate. I want to go home to my fields.*

A red stroke crossed out most of the names. In a second column, someone had recorded how each person died: executed for betrayal; accused of leaking a secret manual; murdered, then declared a victim of qi deviation. These people had not died pursuing power. They had been killed for trying to leave it behind.

Yeonhwa opened her ledger and began to copy.

“All of them?” Jin Cheol asked.

“They died because someone erased their names.”

As she finished the last entry, her iron stylus slipped into a fine groove beneath the nameplate. A loom shuttle answered her words.

No loom stood in the archive, yet the sound came again—wood slipping between warp threads, the treadle’s familiar creak, her mother’s voice scolding her for a bad knot. In the metal wall, another Yeonhwa worked the loom with five whole fingers. Without meaning to, Yeonhwa raised her left hand and laid its three remaining fingers over the reflected hand.

The shuttle flew cleanly through the shed. Too cleanly.

Her mother would have smacked her between the shoulders for weaving at that speed and making the cloth weep. Yeonhwa’s mouth hardened. She seized the phantom shuttle and snapped it across her knee. Only light broke in her hands—but when it did, she saw Jin Cheol pass beside her, walking toward a forge that was not there.

He heard a hammer.

Once. Twice. His father always struck just off the anvil’s center. From beyond the bellows came the scrape of a supper tray and his mother asking when he and Yeonhwa meant to set the wedding date. The sounds were so exact that the next door seemed certain to open onto the afternoon before the disaster.

The metal walls rippled. A forge gathered around him: his father by the bellows, his mother smiling, the hot weight of an axe head held between his tongs. No one had died yet.

This time he could hear the mountain’s warning sooner. If he ran now, he could pull his parents outside. He could send Yeonhwa ahead. Perhaps all four of them could shelter behind the quenching vat.

He took one step.

“Into the water, son. You’ll let the iron cool.”

His father’s voice stopped him more surely than a hand.

Jin Cheol could not lower the axe head into the vat. He needed to hear the iron as it had rung that day, to see what pattern the water made. One more look might show him the thing he had missed.

A palm cracked across his cheek.

Yeonhwa stood behind him, her right hand flushed from the blow. “Listen to the living, not the dead.”

For an instant, all five fingers of her left hand were whole. Light shivered over the wall, and five became three.

“I could do it again,” he said.

“No. You could watch it again.”

“This time I might save them—”

“Then go.” Her teeth showed. “Spend the rest of your life happy inside a memory you made. I’ll take my three fingers outside and count how many living people need to eat today.”

The mountain’s roar drew closer to the illusory forge. Before the metal wall stood a striking block scarred by other visitors’ hammers, and beside it a catch made to break only once. Jin Cheol turned his back on his parents. He heard them die again as he brought his hammer down.

The catch snapped. Resonance tore through the chamber; his father’s voice stretched into a thin metallic cry and vanished. The forge collapsed into cold walls, and behind them the iron door slammed shut.

Yeonhwa had already broken her own illusion. Seo Muhan had not. He knelt in a corner, apologizing to a child no one else could see, and only opened his eyes after they seized an arm each and dragged him upright.

“The price of passing was the way back,” he said hoarsely.

Beyond him, the next door stood open.

A single book rested at the center of the inner archive. Its pages were thin leaves of iron sewn together with cord. Four characters had been cut deep into the cover.

*The Sutra of Unraveling.*

Jin Cheol distrusted the title at once. A manual promising easy strength did not belong among the names in the outer room. The first iron leaf confirmed his suspicion with a warning.

*You who would unravel power: do not touch it while ignorant of power. Closed eyes cannot see the grain. A slow body cannot reach it. Empty hands cannot move it.*

The opening chapter taught only what a beginner could use. The Mind’s Eye sensed the broad movement of qi. Unraveling Qi refined ambient qi into neutral internal energy that carried no sect’s nature. The Form-Shifting Art moved the body half a step aside from a completed collision, beginning with a footwork method called Drifting Step. At the first stage, Perception, Unraveling Qi remained inside the practitioner. It could support the senses and the body’s alignment; it could not reach into anyone else.

The later iron leaves were closed beneath four headings: Analysis, Interference, Synthesis, Dissolution. Most of their contents would remain inaccessible for years, perhaps forever. Interference—the first stage capable of touching another person’s qi circulation—required at least a first-rate body. Dissolution, which claimed it could safely unravel a dantian, lay beyond the bodily transformation of the transcendent realm.

Today Jin Cheol was still slower than Do Changsu. He could take power from no one.

“Why are you smiling?” Yeonhwa asked.

Only then did he notice the pull at the corner of his mouth. “At least it isn’t lying about making me a master by nightfall.”

“This isn’t cultivation severance,” he said after another look at the closed leaves. “It is a road that might one day reach it.”

“A road you may never finish,” Seo Muhan said.

The first training passage began with an injury. To reach Perception, a practitioner had to open a small wound in a soul that had already closed. Common initiation methods used terror to rip such a wound open. This method required the practitioner to find the grain of an existing scar and part it by conscious choice, then filter the ambient qi that entered with each breath until it became Unraveling Qi.

The wound inside Jin Cheol had opened at Cheonggye Village and healed again. According to the sutra, people who retained such a trace were rare. Opening it a second time might return them to the pain that had made it.

Yeonhwa shut the book.

“You don’t have to do this.”

“I do.”

“A moment ago, you condemned men who tore children open to make martial artists.”

“There is a difference between someone else tearing me open and my choosing it.”

“The wound won’t know you chose.”

He had no answer. Heat climbed his scarred back, and his body begged him to stop breathing, close the leaves, leave the mountain. His hands would not release the book.

He opened it again. “Then record it. Write down why I started and where I went wrong. If I go wrong, make me stop.”

Yeonhwa did not reach for her ledger. “If I tell you to stop, will you?”

“I’ll listen to your reasons.”

“You still intend to make the final judgment.”

“It is the most I can promise now.”

She stared at him for a long time, anger held so still it made the silence feel narrow. Then she turned to a blank page.

“I am not giving you permission. I cannot prevent you from training, so I am setting terms. I keep the original iron leaves. You may copy the first chapter and nothing more. Seo Muhan keeps the resonance implement whenever you are not training.” Her stylus touched the page but did not move. “Before you use that implement against a person, Seo Muhan and I must agree, and so must more than half of the people who were harmed. They hear what you plan to do and when you must stop before they decide.”

“And if I break the terms?”

“You lose the common fund, our lodging, and the information network. We take back the manual. If you continue after that, you continue alone.”

Seo Muhan nodded. Jin Cheol pushed the original sutra across to Yeonhwa.

“I’ll begin on those terms.”

He sat for his first cultivation session. Seo Muhan showed him where to begin, then withdrew; this art had no living master, only a book that promised nothing.

Rather than forcing his breath deeper, Jin Cheol remembered the tremor that had crossed the anvil just before the disaster and searched for an echo farther inside. His burns grew hot. Below his navel, a pain as fine as a needle point stirred.

Behind that pain waited the afternoon his parents died.

He did not turn away. The roar returned first, then boiling water, then the smell of burning flesh. He named none of it. He counted instead: breathe in for three, hold for two, release for five. The wound that had sealed inside him parted by the smallest degree.

Cold mountain qi pressed through. When he grasped for it, pain flared across his belly. He shortened the end of his inhalation and let the exhalation run longer. Heat and cold dispersed with the breath, leaving one hair-thin strand beneath his navel, neither warm nor cold and bearing no nature he knew.

His first Unraveling Qi.

Sweat soaked him from scalp to heel. He braced both hands on his knees and vomited. When he finally raised his head, no hidden current illuminated the room. No master’s presence shone through the walls. He could sense only the metal seal trembling at once against his palm and somewhere below his navel.

Seo Muhan took his pulse. “Not even the threshold of third-rate. The flow breaks after three breaths.”

Yeonhwa wrote exactly what he said. *One strand of Unraveling Qi. Three breaths. Vomited once.*

Only after Seo Muhan allowed it did Jin Cheol bring out the three nails recovered from his parents’ remains. At a small brazier in the archive, he bound them to a single ring without dulling their different notes. The crude object was no weapon. It was a training implement: while circulating Unraveling Qi inside his body, he would test which nail’s tremor reached him first.

He had nearly finished when a carrier pigeon came beating up from below the mountain. Yeonhwa untied the message from its leg. The note bore the mark of Mulle Valley’s information network.

*Do Changsu demands double the protection fee. In three days, he will take grain and one child.*

Her face hardened.

Jin Cheol tried the opening stance of Drifting Step. His years at the forge pulled his feet too wide; one attempt woke the pain in his ribs, and the thread of Unraveling Qi scattered.

“We can be down in three days,” he said.

“When will you train?” Seo Muhan asked.

“I’ll be breathing on the way.”

They left the Archive of Forsaken Obsessions before sunrise. The original iron leaves traveled in Yeonhwa’s pack, the resonance implement separately in Seo Muhan’s medicine case. Jin Cheol carried only his copy of the first chapter inside his robe. He stepped into the first half-stride of Drifting Step as the black silver grass brushed their separate burdens and bent them all in one direction.
